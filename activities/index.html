Can a Language Model Hold the Middle? Lessons from Building LLMediator

Jinzhe Tan

LLMediator is a platform we have been building at the Laboratoire de cyberjustice | Cyberjustice Laboratory to help parties in high-volume, low-intensity civil disputes work through mediation. In this talk, I walk through how the system works, including a module that reconstructs the facts and asks parties for missing evidence, a voice agent that carries messages between parties, and simulated disputants we use to test the system at scale. I share what our evaluations show about where large language models help and where they still fail as neutral third parties. I close with open questions about how to evaluate AI tools in dispute resolution and who should be accountable when they get it wrong.

Community learning calls

Our bi-monthly Learning Calls serve as a dynamic space for members to share emerging work, bridge disciplinary gaps, and build a “living directory” of expertise. Whether you’re looking to showcase a new project or find synergies with peers in niche fields, these calls are designed to turn individual insights into collective intelligence.

Why join? Our primary goal is to move beyond polished final results and embrace the process of discovery.

  • Foster early-stage feedback: We cultivate a safe environment for raw ideas, helping you refine your work long before it reaches a final draft.
  • Discover synergies: Identify overlaps in research and projects across the community that you might not have known existed.
  • Cross-disciplinary mapping: Break out of silos and see how your niche fits into the broader ecosystem.
  • Showcase interesting research: We give a stage to the “hidden gems” of the community, ensuring diverse voices are heard.
  • A living directory: By participating, you help us map out who is active in what topic, making it easier for everyone to find the right person for the right conversation.

The format We keep our sessions structured yet conversational. Each one-hour call will start with an input presentation by the presenter, followed by a discussion guided by the moderator and an open Q&A session.

Some ground rules To maintain the integrity of our discussions and protect the “early-stage” culture, we ask all participants to respect the following:

  • Live experience only: These calls are not recorded. We want people to feel comfortable sharing half-baked ideas without fear of them living forever on the internet. (In rare cases, a presenter may choose to record their own 15-minute input, but the discussion remains private.)
  • Humans only: These calls are open to anyone who wants to join—provided they are human. No AI meeting bots/notetakers allowed. We value your presence, not your transcript.

Ready to present your work or join the next session? Reach out to Aurelia, Konrad, or Lucas on Slack or via email to get you started.

Deep-dive debates

The Deep-Dive Debates are our bi-monthly high-intensity sessions designed to foster engaging discussions on the most pressing topics of our field. These are not passive webinars; they are collaborative “think tank” spaces where the European community gathers to pinpoint where current laws (like the AI Act or DMA) lack technical grounding—and where emerging tech lacks legal guardrails.

Why join? Come join our deep-dive debates if you want to debate with us and discuss unsolved problems. Within these debates, we all come prepared having done the mandatory pre-reads. We discuss possible frictions within CS&Law and new issues that emerge.

The format To keep the focus on high-level discourse, we’ve stripped away the fluff:

  • Pre-read: A mandatory, maximum 2-page briefing sent to all participants in advance.
  • Provocation (5–10 min): The session lead provides brief opening thoughts and poses 3–5 “provoking questions” to kick off the debate.
  • Open debate: A structured, moderated discussion governed by our Rules of Engagement.

Rules of engagement To maintain a “frontier” environment that is both rigorous and respectful, all participants agree to the following:

  • Respect the disciplinary gap. If you use jargon—like “proportionality” in law or “differential privacy” in CS—be prepared to define the core concept for the other side.
  • While disagreement is encouraged the sessions are still very much constructive and we aim to try to understand each other.
  • To keep the bar high, organizers reserve the right to refine or decline topics that lack sufficient interdisciplinary friction.

Do you have a topic that needs a Deep-Dive? Reach out to Konrad, Aurelia, or Lucas on Slack or use the channel deep-dive debates to propose a topic.

Under the Hood: What Can We Learn from Leaked System Prompts?

Konrad Kollnig, Lucas Uberti-Bona Marin

When Meta’s agent Muse was asked to archive its files to Google Drive, it packaged 6.8 GB of its own internal runtime environment, complete with system files and credentials. At the same time, ongoing leaks of system prompts give us insight into the complex scaffolding AI companies use to steer and constrain their autonomous agents.

Rather than treating these incidents merely as security slip-ups or IP headaches, we explored what they reveal about the real boundaries of agent isolation, algorithmic transparency, and how “natural language guardrails” interact with technical and legal accountability. We dissected two cases from a leaked system prompts archive and asked: do these leaks expose the limits of prompt-based governance, or do they offer a necessary window into algorithmic accountability?

Pre-read: the Muse runtime export incident. Discussed during the session: system prompts archive.

Cleaning the Launderette: Lessons from 3 years of the European Open-Source AI Index

Dick Blankvoort, Andreas Liesenfeld

Drawing on three years of the European Open-Source AI Index, this talk outlines key learnings about openness in the AI ecosystem. We examine key differences between different types of open-source projects, explore the extent to which ‘open-source’ is used merely as a label, and highlight several of the most prominent examples of open-washing across the open-model ecosystem. We highlight several promising future directions in operationalizing open source as a concept, and point to ecosystem analysis as critical in the future development of the field.

26 June 2026  ·  Community Learning Call

Platform Republics: How Platform Rules Shape the Content of App Licenses

Luka Nenadic

Joint work with Stefan Bechtold, Kevin Davis, and Reinhold Kesler.

Contract law scholarship has analyzed the effect of defaults and mandatory provisions on contracts both theoretically and empirically. We analyze the effects of such rules when they emanate from a private party rather than the state. Using the Apple App Store as a salient example, we show how the platform provides default contractual terms to app developers, which they can use as the End-User License Agreement (“EULA”) of their app. The developers are allowed to customize these agreements, subject to various mandatory terms that Apple requires them to set out expressly in their licenses.

Analyzing an extensive dataset of nearly one million apps with statistical and natural-language processing techniques as well as OpenAI’s GPT-5.4, we find that over 97% of the apps retain Apple’s default terms, and that sophisticated developers are most likely to customize their EULAs. We also find that among all EULA-customizing apps, less than 10% fully comply with Apple’s requirements concerning mandatory terms. Many deviations from Apple’s default and mandatory terms appear to favor the interests of developers, while others constitute clear mistakes. Our results illuminate how the shift of rule-making power from the state to digital platforms shapes contracting through default architectures and the strategic design and enforcement of mandatory rules. More broadly, they demonstrate the promises of platform contractual ecosystems as a laboratory for examining global contractual networks, opt-outs, and standardization.

How Should Law Engage with Other Disciplines?

Arianna Rossi, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux (moderators)

How should law engage with other disciplines — and what does interdisciplinarity really mean for legal scholarship? This debate centred on Alexandra Mercescu’s piece “Turning the Page, Endlessly: Interdisciplinarity in Law”.

The article reflects on how disciplines emerged historically and asks what happens when legal scholarship, often strongly attached to its autonomy, enters into dialogue with other fields. It raises important questions about the promise, limits, and challenges of interdisciplinary work in law.
